# Acquisition Channels

Most companies are dominated by **one or two** acquisition channels. The job is to find
which channel fits your product and model, then concentrate — not to spread thin.

## 1. Product-channel fit comes first

You build the product to fit the channel; you cannot bend a channel to your product.
Before scaling a channel, ask whether the product is *shaped* for it:

- **Virality/referral** needs a product with natural multi-user or shareable output.
- **SEO/content** needs high search intent and content that ranks and converts.
- **Paid** needs a landing→value path that converts cold traffic and LTV that pays back
  CAC fast enough.
- **Sales** needs a deal size that can fund a rep.

A misfit channel underperforms no matter how well you execute it. If a "good" channel
isn't working, suspect product-channel fit before blaming the channel.

## 2. Channel selection — don't boil the ocean

1. **List candidate channels** (SEO/content, paid search, paid social, virality/referral,
   sales/outbound, partnerships/integrations, community, marketplaces/app stores,
   influencers, offline).
2. **Score each** on fit with your product, model economics, and time-to-signal.
3. **Test the top 2–3** with small, time-boxed experiments — enough spend/effort to get
   a real read, not a token trial.
4. **Double down** on the one that shows a working loop and economics; park the rest.

Channels **saturate and decay** — CAC rises as you scale and as competitors pile in.
Keep a "next channel" in exploration even while your main channel works.

## 3. Owned / earned / paid

- **Owned** (SEO, content, email list, community, product virality) — compounding,
  slow to start, cheap at scale. The most durable engines.
- **Earned** (PR, word of mouth, reviews) — high trust, low control, spiky.
- **Paid** (search, social, display) — instant, controllable, but rented: stops when
  you stop paying, and is only a *loop* if revenue funds more spend profitably.

Bias toward building at least one **owned, compounding** channel; use paid to
accelerate, not to be the whole engine.

## 4. Unit economics — the gate on any channel

A channel only scales if the math works:

- **CAC** = fully loaded spend ÷ new customers acquired (include creative, tools, team).
- **LTV** = gross-margin contribution per customer over their lifetime.
- **LTV : CAC** — a common healthy target is **≥ 3:1**. Below ~1:1 you lose money on
  every customer.
- **CAC payback period** — months to recover CAC from gross margin. For SaaS, **< 12
  months** is healthy; shorter is safer because it frees cash to reinvest (this is what
  makes a paid loop actually loop).

Segment economics by channel and cohort — a blended CAC can hide one channel quietly
losing money.

## 5. Scaling discipline

- Watch **marginal CAC**, not average — the cost of the *next* customer rises as you
  scale a channel.
- Protect **quality**: cheap traffic that doesn't activate/retain is negative growth.
  Judge channels on *retained/paying* users, not raw signups.
- Attribute honestly; last-click over-credits bottom-funnel channels and starves the
  top of funnel that created demand.

## Checklist

- [ ] Product-channel fit assessed before scaling any channel.
- [ ] Candidate channels scored; top 2–3 tested with real (not token) budget.
- [ ] At least one owned, compounding channel being built.
- [ ] CAC, LTV, LTV:CAC, and payback tracked **per channel and cohort**.
- [ ] Judging channels on retained/paying users, not raw signups.
- [ ] A "next channel" in exploration before the current one saturates.

## Common traps

- Spreading effort across many channels and winning none.
- Scaling a channel with great top-of-funnel numbers but poor downstream retention.
- Blended economics hiding an unprofitable channel.
- Treating paid as an engine when payback is too slow to compound.
- Blaming the channel when the real issue is product-channel fit.
